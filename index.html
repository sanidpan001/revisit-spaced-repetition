The complete guide to spaced repetition study planning

Every student knows the sinking feeling of opening an exam paper and realising that the chapter they "finished" three weeks ago has quietly evaporated from memory. It is not a failure of intelligence or effort. It is a failure of scheduling. Human memory does not hold information just because we once understood it — it holds information we have been made to retrieve, repeatedly, at the right moments. A spaced repetition study planner is a tool that decides exactly when those moments should be, and Revisit is a free one that builds the whole timetable around the days you can actually study.

Why you forget: the forgetting curve

In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus ran painstaking experiments on his own memory and discovered a brutal law: newly learned information decays exponentially. Within twenty minutes you may have lost a third of it; within a day, most of it is gone. The curve he drew — steep at first, then flattening — is now called the forgetting curve, and every modern study method is an attempt to beat it.

The good news Ebbinghaus also found is that each time you successfully recall something, two things happen. First, retention jumps back up near one hundred percent. Second, the memory trace gets stronger, so the next decay is slower. After several well-timed recalls, the curve flattens so much that knowledge survives for months. Spaced repetition is simply the discipline of scheduling your reviews at the expanding intervals where each recall does the maximum work: not so soon that the review is wasted, not so late that you have forgotten everything and must relearn from scratch.

The SM-2 algorithm, explained simply

The most famous scheduling algorithm for spaced repetition is SM-2, developed by Piotr Woźniak for the SuperMemo software in the 1980s. Its logic is elegant. The first review happens one day after learning. The second happens six days later. After that, each interval is multiplied by an "easiness factor" — a number that starts at 2.5 and is nudged up or down based on how well you recalled the material.

Recall is graded on a scale from 0 to 5. A perfect, effortless recall (5) pushes the easiness factor up and stretches the next interval further into the future. A failed recall (below 3) resets the count: the item goes back to a one-day interval, because the memory trace was never properly formed. This feedback loop is what makes SM-2 adaptive. Easy material automatically gets fewer, wider-spaced reviews; difficult material automatically gets more, tighter ones. Revisit uses this exact SM-2 engine to lay out your exam revision timetable, so the plan adapts to how confidently you rate each topic.

Why ordinary timetables fail busy students

Search for a study schedule generator and most tools hand you the same rigid template: two hours every evening, seven days a week. That is fantasy for most real students. You might have coaching classes on weekdays, a part-time job, family commitments — or you might only be genuinely free on weekends. A plan that schedules reviews on days you cannot study is a plan you will abandon by Wednesday, and an abandoned plan teaches nothing.

Revisit was built around this reality. Its scheduler takes your actual availability as the hard constraint: you mark which days of the week are free and how many hours you can give on a free day, and every single review session is placed only on those slots. If a review is ideally due on a Thursday but you are only free on weekends, Revisit moves it to the nearest free day with spare capacity and keeps the SM-2 ordering intact. Students who study only on Saturdays and Sundays get a plan that is genuinely executable — which, as any teacher will tell you, beats a "perfect" plan that is never followed.

Confidence-weighted revision: study where it hurts

Not all topics deserve equal time. Spending forty minutes re-reading a chapter you already know cold, while the chapter that terrifies you gets the same forty minutes, is one of the most common ways students waste revision season. Revisit asks you to rate your confidence in each topic from 1 (very shaky) to 5 (rock solid) and then does two things with that rating.

First, the review intervals themselves adapt: low-confidence topics get a denser SM-2 sequence with extra reinforcement reviews in the first week, while high-confidence topics get a sparser sequence that simply keeps them warm. Second, the total pool of review sessions is divided by weight — a confidence-1 topic receives roughly five times the slots of a confidence-5 topic. The "smart slot allocation" panel on your plan shows this split openly, so you can see exactly where your hours are going and adjust your ratings if a topic surprises you.

What to actually do in a review session

A schedule is only as good as the sessions in it, and spaced repetition has one golden rule: retrieve, don't re-read. Close the book and force yourself to recall the material — write out the derivation, explain the concept aloud as if teaching it, or answer practice questions from memory. Re-reading feels productive because it is fluent, but fluency is not memory; the slight struggle of retrieval is precisely what strengthens the trace.

Two more evidence-backed habits multiply the effect. Interleaving means mixing topics within a study day rather than drilling one chapter for hours; it feels harder, but exam performance improves because you practise telling concepts apart. Elaborative self-testing means asking "why" and "how" while you recall — connecting a new fact to something you already know builds extra retrieval paths. If a review session feels too easy, that is useful data: raise the topic's confidence rating next time and let Revisit stretch its intervals.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is rating confidence aspirationally instead of honestly. If you mark everything a 4, Revisit will trust you and schedule sparse reviews — garbage in, garbage out. Rate for the student you are today, not the one you hope to be. The second mistake is skipping the early reviews because "I still remember it." That feeling is the peak of the sawtooth, right before the decay; the early reviews are the cheapest ones you will ever do and they buy the most long-term strength.

The third mistake is treating the plan as a prison. Life happens: a session gets missed. The healthy response is not guilt but a small repair — do the missed review the next free day, and if you blanked on the material, drop that topic's confidence by a point so future reviews tighten up. Spaced repetition is forgiving by design; one missed session costs far less than abandoning the system entirely.

Frequently asked questions

What is a spaced repetition study planner?

A spaced repetition study planner schedules your review sessions at expanding intervals — for example 1 day, then 6 days, then 15 days after first learning — based on the science of the forgetting curve. Each review happens just as you are about to forget, which rebuilds the memory stronger than re-reading. Revisit automates this scheduling for all your exam topics at once.

How does the SM-2 algorithm decide my review dates?

SM-2, the algorithm behind Revisit, starts new topics with reviews after 1 day and 6 days, then multiplies each following interval by an easiness factor (starting at 2.5). Topics you rate with low confidence get a denser sequence with extra early reinforcement; topics you rate highly get wider spacing. If you fail to recall something, its interval resets to 1 day so the memory is rebuilt properly.

How many days before my exam should I start?

More is better, but Revisit works with whatever runway you have. With 30+ days, the SM-2 intervals can fully expand and you will see the classic tapering into light final reviews. With under two weeks, the scheduler compresses the early, high-value reviews (days 1, 3 and 6) into your available slots, which is still far more effective than cramming everything the night before.

I can only study on weekends. Will this still work?

Yes — that is exactly what the availability scheduler is for. Mark only Saturday and Sunday as free, and Revisit places every learn and review session on those days, keeping SM-2 ordering and never overloading a day beyond the hours you set. The intervals stretch to fit your real week instead of an imaginary one.

What should I do during a review session?

Retrieve, don't re-read. Close your notes and recall the material from memory — solve problems, write explanations, or answer flashcards. Mix topics within a session (interleaving) rather than drilling one chapter for an hour. If a review feels effortless, raise that topic's confidence rating so future intervals stretch further.
